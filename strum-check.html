Strum check

Strum all the open strings once. Twangle shows every string at the same time: flat, in tune or sharp.

Check every string at once

  1. Tap the tools button above the strings (three tiles and a plus) and pick Strum check.
  2. Strum all the open strings, without pressing any, and let them ring.
  3. Each string gets a short scale with a marker: up is sharp, down is flat, the middle is in tune. Under it, how many cents it is off, a check mark when it is in tune, or a dash when Twangle didn't hear it.
  4. The line under it says how many strings to tune, or All strings in tune.

Then tune the ones that are off

Tap a string, on the row along the bottom or its column in the check, and Twangle goes back to the tuner with that string picked, so you can tune it. Pick Strum check again from the tools button to check the whole instrument once more.

It is the quickest way to see whether your instrument is still in tune between songs, or which string slipped.

Good to know

  • Strum firmly and let the strings ring: the first, loud moment of a strum counts most.
  • A string with a hollow marker is less sure. In standard tuning that is often the B and the high E: their notes overlap with the low E's harmonics, so Twangle can't hear them as clearly. If one looks off, tap it and tune it on its own.
  • A strum check counts a string as in tune by the same rule as the tuner: within 5 cents, or 2 when Settings → In-tune precision is set to Precise.
  • It works with your tuning, whatever it is, on every instrument but the 12-string guitar: its twelve strings ring too close together to tell apart.
  • Switching to Chromatic, or tapping the Tuner tab, ends the strum check.
For the curious: hearing six strings at once

A single string is easy: its wave repeats, and the time it takes gives the note. In a strum all the waves are on top of each other. But a string's sound is more than one note: it is a row of harmonics at one, two, three, four times its pitch. Twangle knows which strings your tuning has, so for each string it looks in the sound's spectrum for that row of harmonics near where the string should be, and measures exactly where they are. It does this on about a third of a second of sound, ten times a second while the strings ring, and weighs the readings by how loud they were.

Some harmonics of different strings land on almost the same pitch. In standard tuning the B string is almost exactly three times the low E's pitch, and the high E four times, so every harmonic of theirs is also one of the low E's. Where two strings share a harmonic, Twangle leans on the ones that don't, and when none are left, it reads the shared one and marks the string less sure.
